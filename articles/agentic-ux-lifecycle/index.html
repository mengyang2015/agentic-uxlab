The Agentic UX Lifecycle: How to Design Agents Across Prepare, Run, and Improve.

By Meng YangIndependent researchFoundational essay

AI agents don't just respond to requests. They can plan steps, use tools, and take actions on our behalf. That changes what we need to design.

With a traditional interface, a person often performs each step. With an AI agent, the person may set a goal and let the system do much of the work. But people still need to know what the agent can do, when to trust it, when to step in, and whether the result is correct.

Agentic UX is about designing that whole relationship—not just the chat screen or the agent's first response.

To make this easier to discuss and design, I've developed the Agentic UX Lifecycle: a practical framework organized around three phases.

Prepare → Run → Improve. The model has one lifecycle and two different rhythms: Run continues as the agent works; Improve happens when evidence suggests something should change.

Give agents autonomy.
Keep humans accountable.

Why do we need a lifecycle for agents?

AI agents create design questions that don't fit neatly into a single interaction. What should people approve before the agent starts? What can the agent do without asking? How should it report progress? What happens if it makes a mistake? And how can the system improve without changing behavior unexpectedly?

These are not entirely new problems. Microsoft's Guidelines for Human–AI Interaction organize design guidance around early interaction, ongoing interaction, errors, and behavior over time. OpenAI's practical guide to building agents emphasizes tool permissions, safeguards, and human intervention for risky actions. Anthropic's Building Effective Agents emphasizes simple designs, transparent behavior, and evaluation.

My lifecycle brings these concerns into one UX journey. It is an original synthesis and a working design tool, not a claim that every agent follows these steps in exactly the same way.

1. Prepare: Make the agent ready to act

Before an agent works on someone's behalf, people need to understand its job and boundaries—and have a way to see whether it behaves as expected.

Define

What outcome should the agent deliver? For whom? What is outside its job? A clear goal is more helpful than a long list of features.

Configure

Give the agent the information, tools, permissions, and boundaries it needs. Make important settings understandable and reviewable.

Test

Let people try realistic examples before relying on the agent. Show what worked, what failed, and what may need attention. A preview is useful; evidence that the agent behaves correctly is better.

Launch

A person approves the first production version. Launch is a decision point, not just a button. It should be clear what is being released and which actions are authorized.

The goal of Prepare: a production-ready agent with a credible reason to trust its initial behavior.

2. Run: Let the agent work without losing control

Once the agent is live, the experience changes. People should not have to watch every small step. But they do need enough visibility to understand progress and respond when attention matters.

Execute

The agent carries out authorized work. It may select tools, take several steps, and adjust its path within defined limits.

Monitor

Show meaningful progress, blockers, and requests for attention. Avoid flooding people with raw logs or every internal step.

Intervene

When necessary, a person can approve a consequential action, redirect the agent, pause it, recover from a failure, or take over.

Verify outcome

Show what the agent actually completed and the evidence needed to judge the result. “Done” should not replace proof of an important action.

The goal of Run: useful work completed with appropriate visibility and control.

3. Improve: Turn real evidence into better behavior

No agent will be perfect on day one. As it operates, failures, unusual cases, and feedback reveal opportunities to improve. But improvement should not mean changing a live agent without review.

Evaluate

Look across results, failures, user feedback, and patterns over time. This is different from checking one completed task.

Adapt

Diagnose why something failed, then change the relevant instructions, tools, rules, or experience. Avoid treating every failure as a prompt-writing problem.

Re-test

Test the proposed change against the original failure and other important cases. A fix should not create a new problem elsewhere.

Release

A person reviews and approves the improved version. The released agent returns to Run, where its effect can be observed. Lessons that hold up over time can also inform future Prepare decisions.

The goal of Improve: better performance without losing track of what changed or who approved it.

Four ways people stay involved

Human involvement does not have to mean constant supervision. Across the lifecycle, I use four simple verbs to describe the meaningful ways people interact with an agent.

These are not four screens. They are four types of human action. Their timing depends on the task, its risk, and the agent's authority.

A practical example: an AI agent answering customer inquiries

Imagine a small business uses an AI agent to respond to missed calls or incoming text messages.

Prepare
The owner defines the goal: answer common questions and collect leads. They connect approved business information and set boundaries: the agent can answer hours and service questions, but cannot promise unavailable appointments. They test common and difficult conversations, review the results, and approve launch.

Run
The agent answers a customer, collects contact details, and records the lead. A dashboard shows the outcome. When a customer asks for an exception the agent is not authorized to make, the agent requests help instead of guessing. The owner can verify that the lead was recorded.

Improve
After several weeks, the team notices that the agent struggles with questions about service areas. They review examples, update the approved information, re-test the difficult conversations, and release the change.

This is an illustrative scenario, not a claim about any particular live product. It shows why the entire journey matters: a well-designed conversation is only one piece of a trustworthy agent experience.

How to use the lifecycle in your own product

Start with a simple exercise. Map your current agent experience to Prepare, Run, and Improve. Then ask five questions:

  1. Value: Can people explain what the agent is supposed to accomplish?
  2. Proof: Can they test important behavior before they rely on it?
  3. Control: Can they see when attention is needed and intervene effectively?
  4. Outcome: Can they verify what the agent actually did?
  5. Change: Can the team improve the agent through a tested, reviewable release?

The model also uses four checks at major approval gates: Fit (right task and context), Behavior (acted as intended), Outcome (acceptable result), and Control (appropriate permissions). These are design questions, not a single magic score.

A framework meant to evolve

The Agentic UX Lifecycle is a starting point for research and design, not a finished rulebook. Agent experiences vary by risk, domain, user, and level of autonomy. Future research may change how we explain specific activities and touchpoints.

At The Agentic UX Lab, I'll use this framework to study real products, compare design patterns, document open questions, and turn findings into practical guidance. The aim is simple: help designers build agents that can do useful work while people retain meaningful accountability.
